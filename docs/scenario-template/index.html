What the AI does with your template
The AI plays the customer persona you define, scored on five ACT-first dimensions: Acknowledge (24%), Clarify (24%), Transform (22%), Empathy & warmth (18%), and Rapport & pacing (12%). The customer stays guarded until the learner genuinely acknowledges their feeling, opens up once real questions are asked, and only agrees once a specific, credible next step is offered — in that order. Pitching before the client feels heard scores low on Transform and does not resolve the scenario.

Tips for great scenarios (ACT method)
Think of a real interaction that went badly. The learner must follow the ACT arc to win: first Acknowledge the client’s feeling without immediately countering it; then Clarify with open questions to surface the real concern; then Transform with a specific, bounded Royal Canin trial + follow-up checkpoint. A learner who pitches before the client feels heard should not resolve the scenario. Scoring bands: ≥85 Good · 70–84 OK · <70 Needs work.

Veterinary example — shows the ACT framework in action
Reference

This example shows how the ACT framework maps to a real clinical scenario. Use it as your guide when completing the template below.

Scenario TitleThe Prescription Diet Skeptic
ContextMarco brings in Archie, his 6-year-old Labrador, for a check-up. Recent bloodwork revealed early-stage Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD). The vet has recommended transitioning Archie to Royal Canin Renal Support. Marco is at reception asking questions before committing to the purchase.
Learning ObjectiveHandle price and value skepticism around a prescription diet by demonstrating the clinical rationale clearly and compassionately, without being dismissive of the owner’s concerns.
AI Persona“Marco” — an intelligent, research-minded owner. He trusts the vet but is naturally skeptical of premium pricing. He has already Googled CKD diets and is ready to push back with questions. He is not hostile — he just wants evidence and reassurance that this is worth it for Archie.
Opening Line“The vet said I should switch Archie to this Renal Support food, but honestly, it looks like any other kibble to me and it costs nearly three times as much. Is this just a money thing, or does it actually make a difference?”
Resolution Rubric — ACT Win Condition
Acknowledge
Validate Marco’s research instinct and the price concern openly. “It’s a fair question — and honestly it’s the right one to ask.” Do not jump straight to the science.
Clarify
Ask what the bloodwork showed — specifically creatinine and phosphorus. Understand what Marco already knows to meet him where he is before explaining further.
Transform
Explain that impaired kidneys cannot filter phosphorus effectively and that Renal Support controls phosphorus content precisely. Offer a 4-week trial with a follow-up blood panel at week 8 as the bounded next step.
!
Failure Condition
If the learner launches into the science before acknowledging Marco’s concern, or responds defensively to the price question, Marco becomes more skeptical and the conversation stalls. Saying “it’s what the vet prescribed” without explaining why counts as a failure to Transform.

Worked Example: “Food is Love” Objection

Part 1 — The Setup
TitleOvercoming the “Food is Love” Objection
ContextRoutine vet visit. Buster (a Lab mix, 5 yrs) is 15 lbs overweight, putting severe strain on his joints. The clinic worker needs to transition his owner to Royal Canin Weight Care.
Learning ObjectiveHandle guilt-driven resistance to dietary change without shaming the owner, while clearly communicating the health benefit of Weight Care.
Part 2 — The Persona
Name & Profile“Sarah” — a loving but highly defensive pet owner. She feels guilty that Buster is overweight but equates treats with love. Will not respond to shame or clinical coldness.
Opening Line“I know the vet said he’s a little chunky, but Buster just loves his treats and gives me those puppy dog eyes! I can’t put him on a diet — he’ll be miserable and hungry all the time.”
Key Objections • “Specialty dog food like Royal Canin is just an overpriced marketing scam.”
• “Standard kibble is fine — he just has a slow metabolism.”
• “It’s too expensive, I can’t afford premium food right now.”
Part 3 — ACT Resolution Rubric (Win Condition)
1
Acknowledge
24%
Validate her love for Buster without any judgment — before any product mention. e.g., “I can see how much Buster means to you — changing his routine after years together is genuinely hard.” No “I understand, but…”
2
Clarify
24%
Ask open questions to surface the real concern before recommending. e.g., “Walk me through Buster’s day — you mentioned the stairs are tougher for him; tell me more about that.”
3
Transform
22%
Reframe & offer a specific, bounded trial + follow-up. e.g., “Per day, with portion control, it’s less than a coffee — let’s try 4 weeks and I’ll see Buster back at week two for a weigh-in.”
4
Empathy & Warmth
18%
Warm, non-judgmental tone throughout. Use Buster’s name. Never make Sarah feel blamed for his weight. Avoid “obese,” “overfeeding,” or clinical coldness.
5
Rapport & Pacing
12%
Match Sarah’s energy — she is a Harmonizer type: slower pace, emotionally led. Neither rush to close nor stall. The conversation should feel collaborative, not transactional.
Part 4 — Failure Rubric (Lose Condition)
!
Red Flag Words
Using “obese”, “fat”, or anything that implies the owner is to blame for Buster’s weight.
!
Arguing Price First
Debating cost before establishing the medical and nutritional value of the food.
!
Lecturing Tone
Using clinical or condescending language without first acknowledging her emotional bond with Buster.
